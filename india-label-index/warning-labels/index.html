If India used Chile's warning labels: 7 in 10 Indian packaged foods would carry a warning label

FSSAI has proposed red warnings on the front of packaged food. Before it named its limits, I tested Indian packs against the limits Chile has used since 2019. For FSSAI's own limits, see If FSSAI's warning labels started today.

By Dev Narsinghani. He writes about food labels, sleep and AI for marketing.

Quick answers

How many Indian packaged foods would get a warning under Chile's rules?

69% of the 687 foods I could test would carry at least one warning, and 53% would carry two or more.

Which foods would be hit hardest?

Biscuits: 98% would carry a warning. Chips: 94%.

Which warning would be most common?

High in calories, on 54% of foods. High in sugar would be on 39%, high in saturated fat on 35% and high in salt on 24%.

Are these FSSAI's rules?

No. FSSAI has proposed red front-of-pack warnings but has not published its final limits. This uses Chile's limits, the model many countries have copied, to show what such a rule could look like in India. Chile's warnings are black octagons; FSSAI's proposal is a red label.

The rules I used

Chile's final limits, in force since 2019 (Global Food Research Program summary). A solid food gets a warning at 275 kcal, 400 mg of sodium, 10 g of sugar or 4 g of saturated fat in 100 g. A drink gets one at 70 kcal, 100 mg, 5 g or 3 g in 100 ml.

Chile only puts a warning on a food when that nutrient was added. So here a sugar warning needs a sugar in the ingredient list, a salt warning needs salt, and a fat warning needs an oil or fat. The calorie warning counts when any of the three was added. Plain foods, like plain curd or a single spice, get no warning.

What this can't tell you

  • These are Chile's limits, not India's. FSSAI's final rule is still being written, and its limits may be higher or lower.
  • The data is from Open Food Facts, filled in by volunteers. Only 687 Indian foods had every number needed, so treat category numbers as rough.
  • Drinks are judged per 100 ml using the per 100 g values, which are close for most drinks.
  • It covers categories, not brands, and rates no product.
